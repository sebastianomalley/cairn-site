In plain words

A companion for the end of life

You've probably heard of birth doulas. They help bring a new life into the world with care and a steady hand. A death doula, also called an end-of-life doula, does the same at the other end of life.

I'm not a doctor or a nurse. I don't give medical care. What I offer is companionship. Doulas call it companioning. It means being with you, listening, helping you plan, and making sure you and your family aren't figuring this out alone.

Part guide, part planner, part calm person in the room.

Day to day

What it can look like

Every family is different. These are just a few examples.

i.

A conversation at the kitchen table

Talking through what matters most, what scares you, and what you'd want if things get harder.

ii.

Getting wishes on paper

Helping you think through your choices and get them to the people who need them.

iii.

Setting the room

The music, the light, the readings or prayers, and who's there. Done your way.

iv.

Sitting through the night

Staying close so the family can sleep, eat or just breathe for a while.

v.

The first hours after

Helping the family through the first hours, honoring the rituals that matter to them.

vi.

Checking in later

Because grief doesn't end when the service does.

Doula and hospice

Side by side, never instead of

Hospice is wonderful, and I work alongside hospice teams, not in place of them. Here's how the two fit together.

Hospice teamEnd-of-life doula
FocusMedical comfort care, like managing pain and symptoms, with nursing visits.Emotional, practical and spiritual companionship for the person and the family.
WhoDoctors, nurses, aides, social workers, chaplains and volunteers.One familiar person who gets to know you and your family.
TimeScheduled visits, with support on call.Unhurried time, including long visits and overnight vigils.
WhenUsually once someone has chosen comfort care near the end of life.Anytime, including years ahead, simply to plan.
AfterBereavement support for the family.Present in the first hours, and checking in during the weeks that follow.
CostTypically covered by Medicare, Medicaid and most insurance.Paid privately for now. Insurance doesn't cover doula care yet, though many of us are working to change that.

Palliative care

Medical comfort care at any stage of a serious illness, often alongside treatment.

Chaplains and faith leaders

Spiritual care within your tradition. I'm glad to work right alongside yours.

Social workers

Help with resources, benefits and the practical systems around care.

Family and friends

The heart of it all. My job is to support them, never to replace them.

Why a doula

Why families bring one in

i.

Time

The kind of long, unhurried time a busy care team often can't give.

ii.

One familiar face

The same person from the first conversation to the last day, and after.

iii.

Care for the whole family

Support for the caregivers, the kids, the far-away relatives, everyone.

iv.

Your wishes, honored

Someone who knows what you want and helps keep it at the center.

v.

Less pressure later

Planning ahead means fewer hard decisions in the hardest moments.

vi.

Someone who isn't afraid of this

A steady presence when everyone else is understandably overwhelmed.

Fair questions

Things people wonder

What if I'm not comfortable talking about death?

Most people aren't, at first. We go at your pace. Sometimes we talk about everything except death for a while, and that's okay too.

Do I need to be religious?

Not at all. I support people of every faith and of no faith. Your beliefs and traditions lead, and I follow.

Isn't it all really sad?

There are tears, sure. There's also a surprising amount of laughter, good stories and the occasional debate about the playlist.

Is it covered by insurance?

Not yet. Doula care is paid privately today, though many doulas are advocating for that to change. We'll talk openly about cost on our first call.

Do you help with pets?

Yes. I'm also certified to help families say goodbye to a beloved companion animal.
